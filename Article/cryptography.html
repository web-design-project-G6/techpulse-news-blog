NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography: Migration to Quantum-Resistant Security

TP
TechPulse News • Sep 29, 2026 • 6 min read

Quantum computing could eventually break some of the public-key cryptography used to protect digital communications today. Because replacing cryptographic systems can take many years, organizations are already preparing for the transition to post-quantum cryptography.

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has finalized new post-quantum cryptography standards and is encouraging organizations to begin migration now. The goal is to replace vulnerable public-key algorithms with cryptographic systems designed to resist attacks from future quantum computers.

1. What Is Post-Quantum Cryptography?

Post-quantum cryptography, or PQC, is cryptography designed to protect information against both traditional computers and future quantum computers.

Instead of depending on mathematical problems that powerful quantum computers could potentially solve, PQC algorithms use mathematical structures that are currently considered resistant to known quantum attacks.

2. Why Does the Migration Matter?

Cryptographic migration is not a simple software update. Organizations may have encryption embedded in applications, websites, databases, hardware, certificates, networks, and cloud services.

Long-Term Data

Sensitive information may need protection for many years, making early migration important.

Complex Systems

Cryptography can exist across software, hardware, networks, certificates, and third-party services.

3. NIST's Main PQC Standards

NIST finalized its first three post-quantum cryptography standards in August 2024. These standards provide organizations with algorithms that can be implemented today.

The Three Standards

  • ML-KEM — used for establishing shared cryptographic keys.
  • ML-DSA — used for digital signatures.
  • SLH-DSA — a hash-based digital signature standard.

4. Migration Is Already Underway

NIST recommends that organizations begin migration to quantum-resistant cryptography now. The process starts with understanding where vulnerable cryptographic algorithms are being used.

Find

Identify applications, systems, devices, and services that use vulnerable public-key cryptography.

Prioritize

Determine which systems and sensitive data should be migrated first.

Replace

Replace vulnerable cryptographic algorithms with appropriate PQC standards.

Test

Test interoperability, performance, certificates, applications, and network protocols.

5. The 2035 Transition Target

NIST's transition planning identifies a long-term move away from quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography. Under the proposed transition timeline, quantum-vulnerable algorithms are scheduled for deprecation and eventual removal from NIST standards.

NIST's current materials describe 2035 as a major endpoint for removing vulnerable public-key algorithms from its standards, with some lower security-strength algorithms facing earlier deadlines.

6. New NIST Work in 2026

The migration effort is continuing in 2026. NIST is working on additional guidance and evaluating additional digital-signature algorithms that could complement the first finalized standards.

  • NIST advanced nine additional digital-signature candidates to the third round in May 2026.
  • NIST released draft updates for PIV standards to support ML-DSA and ML-KEM.
  • Organizations continue testing tools and practices for discovering vulnerable cryptography.
  • Crypto-agility is becoming an important part of migration planning.

7. What Is Crypto-Agility?

Crypto-agility means designing systems so that cryptographic algorithms can be replaced or updated without rebuilding the entire system.

This approach can make future security upgrades easier because organizations can change algorithms, keys, certificates, and cryptographic protocols with less disruption.

8. Challenges to Consider

Moving to post-quantum cryptography requires technical planning and testing. Organizations need to understand both their existing cryptographic systems and the requirements of the new algorithms.

Cryptography Inventory

Organizations need to discover where public-key cryptography is being used.

Compatibility

New algorithms must work correctly across applications, networks, devices, and services.

Performance

Organizations need to test key sizes, signatures, processing time, memory use, and network performance.

9. What Comes Next?

Post-quantum migration is becoming a long-term cybersecurity engineering project rather than a future-only problem. Organizations need to understand their cryptographic dependencies and prepare systems for algorithm changes.

As NIST continues developing migration guidance, organizations can begin building inventories, testing PQC implementations, improving crypto-agility, and planning replacements for vulnerable algorithms.

Key Points

  • NIST has finalized three main PQC standards: ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA.
  • Organizations are encouraged to begin migrating to quantum-resistant cryptography.
  • Cryptographic inventory is an important first step in the migration process.
  • Crypto-agility can make future algorithm changes easier.
  • NIST continues evaluating additional post-quantum digital-signature algorithms.
  • NIST's transition planning targets the removal of quantum-vulnerable algorithms from its standards by 2035.
